Toilet Fill Valve and Flapper Replacement, Oakley, CA

Toilet fill valve and flapper replacement in Oakley, CA is a job most homeowners can finish in under an hour with a few inexpensive parts. A simple dye test tells you which one has failed.

Call (855) 575-2890

By the Oakley 24/7 Plumbing team · Updated October 30, 2026

The flapper is the rubber seal at the bottom of the tank that lifts when you flush. The fill valve is the tall assembly that refills the tank and shuts off at the right level. When either wears out, the toilet runs, hisses or refills on its own.

This page keeps to the parts inside the tank: how to diagnose the leak, how to swap each part, how to set the water level, and when the problem is bigger than a rubber seal.

Find the culprit with food coloring

Lift the tank lid and drop several drops of dark food coloring into the tank water. Do not flush. Wait fifteen to twenty minutes and look in the bowl. Color in the bowl means water is slipping past the flapper, and no color points toward the fill valve or the overflow tube.

Next, look at the water line. If the level creeps up to the top of the overflow tube and spills, the fill valve is not shutting off. If you can hear a constant hiss with the level normal, the valve seat or diaphragm inside the fill valve may be worn. Anti-siphon fill valves are the type the California Plumbing Code, a UPC-based text, expects in a toilet tank.

Before you start swapping parts

Shut the supply and flush

Close the little valve on the wall behind the toilet, flush, and hold the handle to drain the tank. A sponge handles the last bit.

Take a photo and bring the old part

Parts vary by brand and tank size. The old flapper or valve in your hand is the surest way to buy a match.

Look at the flapper seat

Run a finger around the opening. A rough or gritty ring prevents a new flapper from sealing, so wipe it with a nonabrasive pad.

Check the supply line and shutoff

If the wall valve drips or will not turn, stop there. That is a separate repair, and forcing it can cause a leak.

Replacing the flapper in five minutes

Unhook the chain, slip the old flapper's ears off the pegs on the overflow tube, and pull it free. Fit the new one on the same pegs, clip the chain to the handle arm with just a little slack, and set the flapper so it drops squarely onto the seat. Open the supply slowly and watch it fill.

If the old flapper is warped, sticky or covered in a black residue, replace it with a new one made of the same style. Residue that rubs off on your fingers means the rubber is breaking down and will not seal for long.

  • Chain with about a half inch of play so it does not hold the flapper open
  • Match the size, since some tanks take a larger flapper
  • Flush twice and watch for a clean, quick seal

Swapping the fill valve and setting the water level

With the tank drained, disconnect the supply line from the underside, hold the valve steady and loosen the lock nut beneath the tank. Lift out the old assembly, drop in the new one at the right height, and tighten the nut by hand plus a small turn. Reconnect the line, refill and adjust the float until the water stops about an inch below the top of the overflow tube.

Check every connection for drips with a dry paper towel. Hand-tight plus a quarter turn is enough, because porcelain cracks if overtightened.

Many fill valves are adjustable by a screw or a clip on the stem, so you can set the height without cutting anything. Flush once after adjusting and confirm the tank stops where you marked it.

When to stop and call

Call a plumber if the shutoff valve will not close, the tank or bowl has a crack, water appears at the base, or the toilet keeps running after new parts are in. A hidden leak elsewhere can produce the same high bill, so our leak detection visits check the house for other loss. For anything beyond the tank, see toilet repair for the full list of fixes.

Tank parts and Oakley's mineral content

The District's latest report puts average hardness at 124 mg/L (range 87 to 150) and pH between 8.0 and 8.6. Moderately hard water leaves mineral crust on valve seats and diaphragms, so a part that fails in a few years may be scale rather than a bad part. When you replace the flapper, wipe the seat and the underside of the fill valve of white deposits.

A silent leak at the toilet can raise a water bill without any obvious sign. If your bill jumps, the District's phone line at (925) 625-3798 is where to ask about unusual usage, and the dye test above takes five minutes.

Frequently asked questions

Use the dye test. Color showing in the bowl means the flapper or seat is leaking. A hiss with no color points toward the fill valve.
